\section*{Summary}

The question: which learned policy works best inside the xv6 kernel, and does it beat the classical policies (Clock, and now ARC, LHD and
S3-FIFO as well)? Everything in the main text was measured in the real xv6 kernel running on QEMU; the simulator results are in
Appendix~\ref{app:sim}. Numbers are page faults relative to Clock (below 1 is better), averaged with a geometric mean over the streams of each
workload and then over the 14 workloads (so a workload with 17 streams counts once, like one with 2). Memory is 5, 10 and 20\% of each
program's pages. Intervals are 95\% intervals from resampling the workloads, then the streams inside them.

\begin{itemize}
\item \best{Classical policies, in the kernel.} ARC, LHD and S3-FIFO now run in the kernel, in the form a kernel that only sees faults and accessed
  bits can run (Section~\ref{sec:forms}). \textbf{ARC beats Clock at 5 and 10\% memory} (0.947 and 0.943, intervals below 1) and is level at 20\% (0.984). LHD is level at 5\%
  (1.005), and 0.95 at 10 and 20\% with intervals that touch 1. S3-FIFO is not better than Clock at any size (1.09, 1.02, 1.09). FIFO, Aging and LFU are
  worse than Clock at every size (by 4 to 35\%).
\item \best{The linear model.} A small linear score over kernel-visible signals, with whole-number arithmetic, beats Clock at 10\% memory (0.914, interval
  0.85 to 0.98). At 5\% (0.976) and 20\% (0.882) it is better on average but the intervals include 1 (at 20\% only just: the upper end is 1.001): a few workloads decide the average (it is bad on graph and matmul at some sizes, very good on the
  hash join at 20\% memory and on the heap programs).
\item \textbf{Learned against the best classical policy.} The linear model against ARC: 1.031, 0.970 and 0.896 at 5, 10 and 20\% memory; no interval excludes 1. The gap
  moves in the learned model's favour as memory grows. \textbf{We have not shown that a learned policy beats ARC on average}; we have shown that it beats Clock at 10\%, and
  that it is at least level with ARC.
\item \textbf{Heavier models.} The five kinds trained on all workloads (CNN, deeper MLP, set-attention, boosted trees, ranking trees) were run on all 74 streams at 10\% memory
  (and on ten streams at 5 and 20\%). At 10\% the CNN, the MLP and set-attention are level with the linear model (1.000, 0.998 and 0.992; every interval contains 1), and the CNN and the MLP
  are better than Clock (0.914 and 0.912, intervals below 1). Boosted trees are slightly worse than the linear model (1.061, interval 1.01 to 1.12) and ranking trees clearly worse (1.476, 1.05 to 2.42).
  On ten streams at 20\% memory the first three are 12 to 14\% better than ARC, but with intervals that include 1. They cost 20 to 150 times the linear model per eviction
  (Section~\ref{sec:cost}), so we found no reason to prefer them.
\item \textbf{Looking at fewer candidates costs little.} The linear score scanning only 32 randomly chosen candidates per eviction is level with scanning all of them
  (0.998 of the full score, interval 0.975 to 1.017) and still beats Clock (0.912, interval 0.86 to 0.97) at about half the cost (11\,300 instead of 22\,800 instructions at 68 candidates). With 16 candidates it is 1.3\% worse than the full score (1.013, 0.98 to 1.04) and still beats Clock
  (0.926, 0.88 to 0.98) at a quarter of the cost (5\,700 instructions). All 74 streams, 10\% memory.
\item \textbf{Training on all workloads is the fair test.} Trained on one workload, the five kinds are mostly worse than when trained on all (set-attention 1.48 against 0.90 in the simulator);
  models trained on one workload answer a different question and are kept only as a side result.
\item \textbf{The kernel agrees with the simulator.} For the three new policies, kernel faults divided by simulator faults are 0.995 (ARC), 1.014 (S3-FIFO) and 0.984 (LHD), geometric mean over 74 streams at 10\% memory; the median stream is within 1.5\%.
\end{itemize}

\section{What was run}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{The kernel and the metric.} Each run is one program in xv6 with its resident memory limited to a fraction of the program's pages, so every eviction goes through the policy under test.
The count is zero-fill faults plus swap faults. xv6 runs are exactly repeatable (the same program, stream, memory size and policy give the same faults every time); the uncertainty
in the tables comes from which streams were chosen, not from run-to-run noise. 2\,743 runs are merged in one file (\code{report/results2/kernel\_eval\_all.csv}); the long runs were split between the thesis PC and
Kaggle notebooks, and 180 runs repeated on both gave identical faults, evictions and writes.

\textbf{Workloads (14).} B-tree, graph, key-value store (the original and the Twitter replays, ``kv-T''), LZW, matrix multiply, sort, heap churn, hash join, sparse matrix--vector product (SpMV),
pointer chase, Bloom filter, the synthetic pattern suite (\code{pat}) and four application-shaped programs (\code{app}). 74 streams at each of the three memory sizes.
Every program's access string is checked reference by reference against an independent model.

\textbf{Policies in the kernel.} FIFO, Clock, Aging, LFU; the learned linear score (\code{global}: one set of weights for all workloads; a per-workload set exists too);
five heavier models trained on all workloads (the six original ones), converted to whole-number arithmetic; and ARC, LHD and S3-FIFO.

\subsection{The three classical policies in the kernel}
\label{sec:forms}

A kernel does not see every access. It sees faults, and the accessed and dirty bits of a page when it looks. ARC, LHD and S3-FIFO as published react to every hit, so they cannot be
run unchanged. Each was adapted in the same way: the accessed bits are read, and cleared, at each eviction, and a page found accessed counts as having been hit since the last look (the touch that loaded a page is ignored).
Everything else follows the original: for ARC the four lists, the ghost lists and the adaptation of the target size; for S3-FIFO the small queue (a tenth of memory), the main queue and the ghost queue; for LHD the
hit and eviction histograms per class and age, with time counted in evictions. The simulator versions were checked against independent reference implementations
(570 traces, with identical faults and evictions on all). They are named ``ARC'' in the tables, but they are \textbf{not} exactly ARC: the S3-FIFO result in particular (not better than Clock) should be read as
a result for this adapted form, not for the original algorithm.

\section{What choosing a victim costs}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\input{tables/cost}
\caption{Instructions the kernel spends choosing one victim, with 68 candidates (a 64-frame limit), counted exactly in QEMU's instruction-counting mode (repeatable to under 0.3\%), and the same as a multiple of the linear model.}
\end{table}

Counts are exact, not timings. The linear score costs about 22\,800 instructions per eviction at 68 candidates (about 340 per candidate), 55 times Clock. ARC and LHD cost about half to two thirds of that (11\,900 and 14\,200),
S3-FIFO 1\,400. The heavier models cost 20 times (boosted trees, ranking trees), 79 (set-attention), 87 (CNN) and 150 times (deeper MLP) the linear score. So ARC is the cheaper of the two best policies, and its advantage over the
linear score at 20\% memory is the thing to watch; the heavier models cost far more than they could save unless a page fault is very expensive. Whether a saved fault pays for the extra work depends on two things we have now measured on the thesis PC. \textbf{Device latency} (4\,KB random reads, one request at a time, bypassing the file cache): a Samsung 980 NVMe SSD, median 63 \textmu s (99th percentile 134 \textmu s), and a WD Blue 1\,TB hard disk, median 8.9 ms (99th percentile 24.5 ms). The hard-disk figure is for reads spread over a 1\,GB file; a compact swap area (xv6's is 32\,MB) would see shorter seeks and a lower figure, so 8.9 ms is a pessimistic value for a hard disk (a 32\,MB test fits in the drive's own cache and measures the cache, not the disk, so it cannot be used). \textbf{Speed of the scoring code}: the kernel's own scoring headers compiled for this CPU (one core of a Ryzen 5 5600) take 1.35 \textmu s for 68 candidates with the linear score (20 ns per candidate), 33 \textmu s with boosted trees, 106 \textmu s with set-attention and the CNN, and 206 \textmu s with the deeper MLP. Divided by the kernel's instruction counts that is about 0.06 ns per instruction, 17 times faster than the 1 ns we had assumed; adding the kernel's page-table walk that reads and clears the accessed bits (the same three-level walk and the same counters), the linear score takes 1.52 \textmu s with warm caches and 2.11 \textmu s when the program has flushed the caches between two evictions (0.093 ns per instruction); the heavier models change by under 10\%. Using the cold-cache figure for every policy, the linear score is still faster than Clock on the SSD (0.96 of Clock's time, on 9 of 14 workloads) and the CNN, MLP and boosted trees are still much slower (3.9, 5.8 and 1.8). The timing still leaves out the TLB flush after the scan and the locks, so it remains a lower bound.
Time spent on faults and on choosing victims, relative to Clock (geometric mean over workloads; below 1 is faster):

\section{Limits and what is not claimed}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One operating system.} xv6 on QEMU, a teaching kernel with a single-threaded benchmark process at a time. Nothing here says what happens in Linux.
\item \textbf{Time is measured only in parts.} Instruction counts are exact. Device read latency was measured on one SSD and one hard disk (a device read, not a full xv6 fault, and for the hard disk over a 1\,GB file), and the speed of the scoring code on one desktop core, including the page-table walk but not the TLB flush or the locks, so it is a lower bound. How long a real xv6 fault takes on real hardware was not measured.
\item \textbf{Fourteen workloads.} Some are close variants of each other, and the five heavier models were run on only ten streams at 5 and 20\% memory (all 74 at 10\%). The intervals show the resulting uncertainty; most differences between the best policies are inside it.
\item \textbf{Adapted forms.} ARC, LHD and S3-FIFO ran in the form a kernel can run (accessed bits read at evictions), not as published; S3-FIFO's result in particular should not be quoted as the algorithm's.
\item \textbf{``All workloads'' means the six original ones} (btree, graph, kv, LZW, matmul, sort), for the global linear model and the five heavier kinds alike; the heap, hash join, SpMV, pointer chase, Bloom filter, pattern and application programs were not in training. At 5 and 20\% memory the heavier models were run on only ten streams, so their intervals there are the widest.
\item \textbf{The learned policy can be worse than Clock} (matmul at 5\%, SpMV, graph with the global weights at 20\%); the tables show where.
\end{itemize}

LeCaR mixes two experts and learns, from the pages each expert evicted too early, which one to trust. As published it does well on btree and kv (0.90 to 0.92, against 0.95 to 0.99 for ARC and LRU), ties them on graph (0.97 to 0.98; LHD is better there), loses on LZW (1.04), and does not reach the linear score on the kernel's signals (0.80 to 0.90 on the same four scopes). In the form a kernel can run it is worse than Clock on most scopes (1.06 to 1.33 on btree, graph, kv and LZW). On the two streams we looked at, the two experts were penalised about equally often, so the weights drift instead of settling on Clock, and the frequency expert, a count that can only be updated at evictions, is a poor expert on its own (the kernel's frequency policy is worse than Clock on average: 1.07 to 1.27). Both versions were checked against independent reference implementations (95 traces, identical faults and evictions). We did not run it in the kernel.

\section{How to reproduce}
\label{app:repro}

\begin{itemize}
\item Kernel runs: \code{python3 tools/ml2/kernel\_eval.py --fracs 0.1 --nonml arck,s3fifo,lhd}; the model runs use \code{--models cnn-all,\dots}. Results go to \code{report/results2/}; the Kaggle packages are in \code{kaggle\_package/round1..round4}.
\item Merge: \code{python3 tools/ml2/merge\_kernel\_results.py}; intervals: \code{kernel\_ci\_classic.py}; tables: \code{report\_w15.py} then \code{report\_w16.py}.
\item Kernel policies: \code{VM\_POLICY\_ARCK}, \code{S3FIFO}, \code{LHD} (5, 6, 7) in \code{kernel/vmpage.c}; in xv6, \code{vmrun arck|s3fifo|lhd <program>}.
\end{itemize}
