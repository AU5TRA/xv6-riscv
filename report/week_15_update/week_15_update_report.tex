\section*{Summary}

Week 14 showed that a small linear model over signals the kernel can see (accessed and dirty bits and cheap
bookkeeping) beats Clock by 10--15\% in faults on our benchmarks. This week tested how far that result
reaches. All numbers are faults relative to Clock (below 1 is better), averaged geometrically over memory
sizes of 5, 10 and 20\% of each program's pages.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More, harder workloads.} Added the fixed \code{lzw} benchmark to the full pipeline, a synthetic
  pattern suite with known answers (\code{patbench}), replays of real Twitter cache traces, real Redis and
  SQLite page traces, and four application-shaped programs (pointer chase, sparse matrix--vector product, hash
  join, Bloom filter). Every program's access string is checked reference by reference against an independent
  model.
\item \textbf{Literature baselines.} ARC, S3-FIFO, an MGLRU analogue, LHD and exact MRU are implemented in the
  simulator and checked against independent reference implementations. LHD is the strongest untrained policy
  (0.90--0.93 on our data); ARC and S3-FIFO are mixed, and S3-FIFO is worse than Clock on graph, matmul and sort.
\item \textbf{Wider model comparison.} Trees (CART, random forest, boosted trees, ranking boosted trees, logistic regression)
  and neural nets (deeper MLP, set-attention, CNN and LSTM over page history, a CNN over the kernel's own
  scan-bit history, mixed models) were fitted on every scope and selected on validation only. With signals the
  kernel can actually observe, \best{no model class beats the linear scorer by the 2\% and at-most-2$\times$-cost
  bar overall}. Gains of 5--12\% appear on single workloads (kv, the Twitter replays);
  models given oracle information gain up to 6\% in distribution and fail badly on the real Redis and SQLite traces.
\item \textbf{In the xv6 kernel} (157 runs on the new workloads, plus 61 with sampled scoring): the learned
  policy cuts faults by 16\% on lzw (0.84), 15\% on the Twitter replays (0.85) and 15\% on patbench (0.85)
  against Clock, and does nothing on the application programs (1.00). The simulator predicts kernel faults to
  within about 4\%, and the two larger gaps are explained (Section~\ref{sec:gap}). Scoring only 32 random candidates (now in the kernel) costs 0 to 1 point of faults (lzw 0.837 to 0.842, patbench 0.853 to 0.861) and cuts the cost of choosing by a quarter to two thirds; with 16 candidates the linear model beats Clock in total time even on fast flash on lzw (0.91) and the Twitter replays (0.93).
\item \textbf{Five extra models were ported to the kernel and their time delay measured} (Section~\ref{sec:five}). On kv a CNN over the
  aging bits and a deeper MLP are about 2.5\% better than the linear model, but cost 85 and 148 times its instructions per eviction
  (1.7 and 2.9 million instructions against 19\,000); boosted trees equal it at 21 times; on graph the linear model is clearly
  better. Timing is repeatable to under 0.3\% because QEMU's clock is driven by instruction count.
\item \textbf{AU5TRA's programs were merged} (pointer chase, hash join, Bloom filter, sparse matrix--vector, heap churn; Section~\ref{sec:austra}): in our
  kernel each reproduces her model's trace exactly. The linear model gains most on heap programs (kernel 0.65 on \code{heap-mixed}) and fails on SpMV.
\item \textbf{All three memory sizes were run in the kernel} (Section~\ref{sec:sizes}; long runs split over this machine and four Kaggle notebooks, whose results matched this machine
  exactly on 180 runs repeated on both). Over ten workloads the linear model scores 0.95, 0.92 and 0.81 of Clock at 5, 10 and 20\% memory;
  FIFO, Aging and LFU are worse than Clock at every size.
\item \textbf{The two unexplained gaps are explained} (Section~\ref{sec:gap}): the kernel starts from filled memory while the simulator started empty, and set-attention's result
  depends strongly on what is in memory at the start (up to 14 times), so its good simulated result was a lucky case.
\item \textbf{Cost against number of candidates, and the break-even} (Section~\ref{sec:cost}): the linear model pays for itself on a hard disk on every workload with
  a model of its own and about breaks even on fast flash; the CNN, MLP and set-attention do not pay on either, because they cost about 75 to 145 times as much per eviction as the linear model.
\item \textbf{Fairer comparison of the extra models.} Trained on all workloads, as the global linear model is, the CNN and MLP beat it by only 1.8\% and 0.9\% in the simulator (24 cells), and not at all in the kernel at 10\% memory (ten streams: 0.936 and 0.934 against 0.934), at 75 to 145 times the cost.
\item \textbf{A reproducibility bug was found and fixed.} Training samples depended on Python's per-process
  string hashing, so datasets could not be rebuilt identically. All models were refit; Clock, LRU and
  the other classical results are unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\section{What changed in the data and the pipeline}

\textbf{Reproducibility fix.} The sampling seed of each training stream used Python's \code{hash()}, which is
randomised per process, so two builds of the same dataset differed. The seed is now a CRC32 of the stream name, a
capacity and an optional salt. Rebuilding twice is bit-identical (checked over 11 dataset files); all 4\,272
classical result rows of the original streams are unchanged. Because the old caches could not be recovered,
every model was refit, so numbers move slightly from week 14 (for example the global default kernel model now
has five features instead of four). The noise floor from sampling alone is measured in Section~\ref{sec:noise}.

\textbf{lzw.} The corrected lzw benchmark from the team (\code{lzw-r20} for training, validation and test;
\code{lzw-r30} held out) now runs through the whole pipeline: simulator sweep, neural models, selection, test,
kernel export.

\section{Literature baselines}

Implemented in the simulator and cross-checked against independent reference implementations on 475 cases
(identical counts): ARC (integer adaptation), S3-FIFO (as published, and a form that reads accessed bits only
at queue heads), an MGLRU analogue for anonymous memory (generations, aging walk, rescue at eviction), LHD in
a scan form, and exact MRU. MRU equals Belady on cyclic loops (9 of 9 checks), and LRU, FIFO and Clock miss every
access of a loop larger than memory, as theory says. Table~\ref{tab:extra} shows them next to the learned models.

\section{Wider model comparison}

\textbf{Protocol.} The grid was written before any result: kernel-observable inputs (K) or all inputs (FK),
with or without candidate-set rank features; CART (depth 4, 8), random forest, boosted trees (30 and 100 trees,
8 and 16 leaves), ranking boosted trees, logistic regression; eight neural architectures. Candidate
sets come from the same recorded eviction decisions as before; the target is the log of the next-use distance.
Probation is fixed at 2 scans. Selection is on validation streams at 10\% memory only; the chosen models are
then replayed on test, held-out and transfer streams at 5, 10 and 20\%. Intervals are 95\% stream-level
bootstraps. The neural models run as numpy callbacks in the simulator (checked against PyTorch to $10^{-6}$)
and are not run on streams of more than 400\,000 references for cost; the C tree scorer is checked against
scikit-learn and LightGBM to $3\times10^{-6}$. A follow-up added four- to sixteen-tree boosted ensembles to
meet the cost bar.

\textbf{What the wider comparison says.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \best{Richer models help only where they are given information a kernel does not have.} With oracle inputs
  the best tree and neural models are 2--5\% better than the best linear model in distribution; with kernel
  inputs only, trees tie the linear model (btree 0.834 against 0.835, graph held-out 0.765 against 0.798,
  global 0.890 against 0.870) and small boosted ensembles (4--16 trees) are \emph{worse}: lzw 1.19 and
  1.57 against 0.82 and 0.81 for the linear model.
\item The deployable neural nets (deep MLP, set-attention, aging-bit CNN) gain 5--6\% on kv and 2\% on btree
  and lose 2--4\% globally. Their kv gain is the one result that passes the 2\% bar on a workload scope, at roughly
  100$\times$ the arithmetic of a linear scorer.
\item \textbf{Robustness is the real difference.} On the Twitter and application streams the kernel-input trees
  beat the linear model on the Twitter replays by 12\% (0.86 against 0.98) and on the application streams by 3.5\%
  (1.04 against 1.08), because they do not extrapolate the way a linear score does; but on the real Redis and SQLite traces every complex model is 22--41\% worse than the linear one.
  None of the extra capacity is free of this trade.
\item matmul has no validation streams (selection used its training streams), and every richer model is
  20--30\% worse than the linear one on its held-out streams: more capacity overfits when there is little data.
\end{itemize}

  starting the simulator from filled memory brings the ratio to 1.00 (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\item The learned policy saves 16\% of faults on lzw (per-workload model; 6\% for the global model), 15\% on the
  Twitter replays and 15\% on patbench, and does not help on the application programs.

  (lzw 0.858, Twitter replays 0.884, patbench 0.873). The sampler is a partial Fisher--Yates over the candidate array and needs no extra memory.
\end{itemize}

\section{The five extra models in the kernel}
\label{sec:five}

The extra models that use only kernel-visible signals were ranked on validation data (no test data used). The best of each of five
kinds were converted to whole-number arithmetic and put into the kernel as built-in models, chosen with
\code{vmrun ml <name>}: a CNN over the aging bits (trained on kv), set-attention (graph), a deeper MLP (kv),
ranking boosted trees (graph) and boosted trees (kv). The integer versions choose the same victim as the original models on
381--399 of 400 recorded decisions, and give the same faults in the simulator to within 0.5\% (one exception: ranking
trees on one graph stream, 13\% more). In xv6 they were run on every kv and graph test and held-out stream and the Twitter
replays at 10\% memory.

\section{Programs from AU5TRA}
\label{sec:austra}

AU5TRA's branch was merged into this one. It adds the sources of five programs: pointer chasing (\code{chasebench}: a linked list,
and a tree with 64- or 256-byte nodes), hash join (\code{joinbench}: uniform keys, Zipf keys, and a four-way partitioned
join), a Bloom filter (\code{bloombench}), sparse matrix--vector product (\code{spmvbench}: random and banded), and heap
allocation and release (\code{heapbench}: small, mixed and churn). It also adds an independent implementation of the patterns I had
called patbench, with different strings; it is kept as \code{patbench2} and its streams are called \code{patb-*}. Her sweep
folder \code{traces3/} held 116 traces, but only 26 different reference strings, because the string of a program does not depend on
the memory limit; the other 90 are copies, and all of her btree, graph, kv, lzw, matmul and sort strings equal ones we already had.

\textbf{Checks.} Her host models reproduce the traces she collected in the kernel exactly (heap, join, SpMV). In our kernel each of the
five programs produces a trace identical, byte for byte, to her model's prediction (short runs; the pointer-chase run is 149\,870 references
and the SpMV run 2\,097\,678).

\textbf{Simulator results} (23 streams, geometric mean of faults relative to Clock, over 5, 10 and 20\% memory): the linear kernel-signal
model 0.85, S3-FIFO 0.93, ARC 0.94, LHD 0.95; the five extra models 0.94 to 1.02. The linear model gains most on heap programs (0.62 to 0.82)
and on hash joins with uniform or Zipf keys (0.73 to 0.80). Nothing helps on the pointer-chase list or the Bloom filter (all 1.00); the best possible policy,
which sees the future, reaches 0.66 there, so there is room, but no online policy finds it. On her patbench the results agree with mine, for example 0.76 against 0.76 on
Zipf 0.99, and the oracle-feature model fails in the same way on moving hot sets (2.4 times Clock).

\textbf{In the kernel} (13 streams at 10\% memory, 143 runs, no failures) the global linear model scores 0.91 against Clock. The heap programs
improve most (0.65 on \code{heap-mixed}). Two of the extra models are dangerous outside their training workload: on the banded SpMV,
set-attention makes 47.7 times Clock's faults and the ranking trees 6.7 times.

\section{Why the kernel and the simulator disagree}
\label{sec:gap}

Two results of the first version of this report were unexplained. Both are now explained.

\textbf{The kernel starts from filled memory; the simulator started empty.} Every benchmark first touches all its pages (the
populate step, not traced) and only then starts the traced part, so the traced part begins with a full memory whose contents the
policy has already shaped. The simulator replayed the traced string from empty memory. Putting one sweep over the pages in front of the string
(its own faults not counted) removes the kv-scope linear model's gap on the Twitter replays: kernel over simulator changes from 0.903 to 0.999.
For the other policies the change is at most 0.02.

\textbf{Set-attention is very sensitive to the starting state.} I replayed 14 streams from five starting states (empty, and after a
populate sweep in ascending, descending and two random orders) and took, for each policy and stream, most faults over fewest.

Clock, the global linear model, the CNN, the MLP and the boosted trees change by 1--5\% (largest spread 1.05). The linear model of one workload changes by
more: matmul 6\%, kv 12\% (the gap above). Set-attention changes by a median of 2.0 times and up to 14.4 times (on one graph
stream its faults go from 84\,820 to 164\,265), and the ranking trees by up to 1.37 times. In the simulator, from the empty start, set-attention looked excellent
on graph (0.50 of Clock on five of the six streams and 0.78 on the sixth); in the kernel it scored 0.91 to 1.30. The empty start was the lucky case, and the kernel
result lies inside the spread. A model whose result depends this much on what happened
to be in memory first should not be trusted from one run.

\section{All three memory sizes}
\label{sec:sizes}

size, policy and model, and the long runs were split over this machine and four Kaggle notebooks. To check that the Kaggle machines give the same results, 180 of their runs were repeated on this machine: the
faults, zero-fill faults, evictions and page writes were identical on all 180. (An earlier version of this report said 261 runs; those were copies of rows each notebook started from, not repeats.)
The Kaggle machines were about 3.1 times slower than this one (median over 61 repeats; quartiles 2.9 to 3.2).

\section{What choosing a victim costs, and when it pays}
\label{sec:cost}

The earlier timing was one stream and one memory size. Here the memory limit is varied: \code{patbench} in uniform mode over 1\,024 pages, with 16, 64, 256 and
512 frames, so that every eviction chooses among that many candidates, for every policy. QEMU runs in instruction-count mode, so the counts do not depend on the
host. The unit is instructions spent in the policy itself (the candidate list and the swap-out wait are not included).

\begin{itemize}
\item The cost of a full scan grows in a straight line with the candidates: about 120 instructions per candidate for Aging and LFU, 360 for the linear
  model, 7\,300 for boosted trees, 27\,000 for set-attention, 30\,000 for the CNN and 51\,000 for the MLP. Clock costs under 1\,100 instructions up to 512 candidates and FIFO under 2\,700.
\item Sampling 32 candidates makes the cost independent of the memory size: the linear model costs 11\,800 instructions at 256 candidates and at 512,

\item The rest of an eviction (finding the candidates and the bookkeeping) is about 6\,000 to 6\,500 instructions for every policy (measured on the kv stream, 57 candidates).

\textbf{Does the saving in faults pay for the extra time?} For each workload I took the faults saved per eviction (from the kernel runs) and the measured
cost of the policy at that run's number of candidates, and computed the \emph{break-even}: the cost of one fault above which the policy wins. The time is
then the faults times their cost plus the evictions times the choosing cost, relative to Clock. Two fault costs are shown: 100~\textmu s (a fast flash
disk) and 8\,ms (a hard disk).

\item \best{The linear model of its own workload pays for itself on a hard disk on every workload that has one} (btree, graph, kv, kv-T, lzw, sort: time 0.58 to 0.94 of

\item The CNN and the MLP never pay: their break-even is 10 to 670 ms per fault, and they are 1.0 to 2.4 times slower than Clock on a hard disk

\item Boosted trees pay only on a hard disk, on kv (0.93 of Clock's time) and kv-T (0.90), and nowhere else.
\item Sampling keeps the linear model's choosing cost from growing with the memory size, and it moves the break-even: with 16 sampled candidates the linear model

\section{A safety guard (prototype, simulator only)}
\label{sec:guard}

The linear model loses to Clock on some workloads (SpMV, parts of matmul and graph). That is a result, not a defect of the comparison; this section is an optional extra that asks whether a guard could stop it. The prototype alternates the two policies on the same memory:
one window of $W$ accesses under the learned policy, one under Clock, compare the faults of the two windows, use the better one for the next $K$ windows, and test again.
It runs in the simulator from a filled-memory start, with the global linear model as the learned policy. I tried window lengths of $n/200$ to $n/50$ of the stream
and $K$ from 4 to 80 on 11 streams (33 cells) and carried three settings over to 18 streams the grid had never seen (54 cells).

\item On the streams used to choose the settings the guard looks very good: the worst cell falls from 1.49 to 1.04 and the cells where the learned policy loses from 1.13 to 1.01.
\item \textbf{On streams it was not tuned on it helps less}: the worst cell falls from 1.86 to 1.36, the cells where the learned policy loses from 1.29 to 1.07, and the average

\section{Limits and what is next}
\begin{itemize}
\item The real Redis/SQLite traces and the Twitter replays are small or lack read/write marks; the Twitter streams are
  hash-sampled to 1\,024 keys, which preserves each key's request sequence but not the original working-set size.
\item Neural models were not scored on streams of more than 400\,000 references in the simulator, so the simulator comparison on those streams covers trees only; in the kernel all five models were run.
\item The kernel runs of patbench and appbench use 50\,000 accesses, not the full 1M, for time.
\item The five extra models were each trained on one workload (kv for the CNN, MLP and boosted trees; graph for set-attention and
  ranking trees). Where they are run on other workloads (Table~\ref{tab:full}) the numbers show how well they carry over, not what
  they could do if trained there. Training them on all workloads was done in the simulator (Section~\ref{sec:five}, 24 cells); their kernel runs at 10\% memory are in Section~\ref{sec:five}; the 5\% and 20\% runs and training on the new programs are not finished.
\item At 5\% and 20\% memory one stream per program variant was run (42 streams), not every seed.
\item Time is counted in instructions, at one nanosecond per instruction, which is conservative for a real processor. The break-even
  figures depend on that and on the cost of a fault on the machine in question, which xv6 on QEMU does not give; two example costs are shown.
\item The week-14 and the current builds of the benchmark programs differ, which moves every policy's fault counts by about 1\%. Results
  were only compared within one build.
\item Not done: training on the new programs; the stronger classical policies (LHD, ARC, S3-FIFO) in the kernel, where they are compared in the simulator only; a measured cost of a real page fault.
\end{itemize}
