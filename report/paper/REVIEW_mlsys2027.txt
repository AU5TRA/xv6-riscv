MLSYS 2027 REVIEW
"Can Lightweight ML-Guided Page Replacement Manage Memory Better Than Classical Policies?"

Reviewed: report/paper/main2.tex at 9c48f14 plus the uncommitted edits of 21 Oct 2026; every point
checked against the repo's data, code or cited papers.

Rating: weak reject. Confidence 4/5. Novelty 2/5, quality 3/5, interest 3/5, impact 2/5.


SUMMARY

The paper adds demand paging and swap to xv6 and builds PageScore, which at each eviction scores 32
randomly sampled resident pages with an integer linear model over five kernel-visible features,
fitted offline by ridge regression to predict log time-to-next-use (imitating Belady). On one
emulated core, over 14 workloads, 74 streams and 5/10/20% memory, it has about 9% fewer faults than
Clock at 10% and 20%, no significant difference from accessed-bit ARC and LHD, and cheaper decisions
than both. Larger models do no better.


STRENGTHS

1. Honest scoping: no claim to beat ARC or LHD; it admits covering a fifth of the gap to Belady,
   lists its failures and reports negative results.
2. Real implementation work: nine policies on one kernel fault path, including integer versions of
   five neural and tree models; exact instruction counts; outputs checked identical under every
   policy.
3. Care with the data: streams checked against independent program models and the simulator against
   the kernel; held-out workloads, two-level bootstrap intervals, a multiplicity correction for the
   primary claim.
4. Useful findings: training on random samples learns to evict the page just loaded, which probation
   fixes (Obs. 3); idle time dominates; extra information is separated from model size.
5. Clear writing and a full artifact.


MAJOR WEAKNESSES

W1. Novelty: the core recipe is LRB's and the sampling argument is LHD's; neither is acknowledged.
- LRB (NSDI'20) "randomly samples cached objects to gain eviction candidates" (64), evicts the one
  predicted to be requested last, and regresses "log(time-to-next-request)". PageScore does the same
  with 32 samples and a linear model, yet §6 lists LRB only among caches that "see every access".
- LHD (NSDI'18): "evictions instead sample 64 random objects", because "the quality of a random
  sample depends on its size, not the size of the underlying population", almost word for word
  §3.3's "A fixed sample". Redis's approximated LRU, Hyperbolic caching (ATC'17) and Psounis &
  Prabhakar (ToN'02) aren't cited for sampling either.
- LearnedCache already runs a learned eviction model in Linux via cache_ext (a non-archival MLSys
  2026 poster plus arXiv). The stated difference, "file pages rather than a program's own memory",
  isn't argued; the real one is visibility: read() and write() reach the kernel, while anonymous
  memory exposes only accessed and dirty bits. Lead with it.
- So "sample and regress log reuse distance" is known; the contribution is its adaptation to
  accessed bits only, integer scoring and the probation and recent-page fixes, plus the study. Say
  so.

W2. The baselines are weaker than needed, and the cost advantage comes from how they were adapted.
- Published LHD samples 64 candidates; the kernel's LHD scores every page (14,397 to 111,705
  instructions from 68 to 516 pages, Table 13). So §4's "everything else follows the published
  algorithm" is false for LHD, and "matches them at a lower cost per decision … a cost that barely
  grows with memory" (abstract, §1, §8) is an artifact of the adaptation.
- The adapted ARC also scans every page per eviction (129,555 instructions at 824 pages). CAR (ARC's
  accessed-bit version, by one of its authors) and CLOCK-Pro (LIRS's accessed-bit form, built on
  reuse distance) are cited in §6 but run nowhere, so "ARC and LHD, the best classical policies a
  kernel can run" (§1, §5.2, §8) is unsupported.
- No Linux-style baseline (active/inactive lists with refault distance, or MGLRU), even simulated,
  though §7 argues transfer because Linux keeps these signals.
- No trivial-predictor baseline (sample 32, apply probation, evict the longest-idle page). Idle
  dominates (Fig. 6), so how much of the 9% is learning, not sampling plus probation, is unknown.
- No best-of-classical oracle, though winners differ by workload: at 10%, LHD reaches 0.63 on graph
  against PageScore's 0.77, and ARC (0.97) and LHD (0.87) beat PageScore (1.10) on matmul (Table 4).
  A selector among Clock, ARC and LHD (LeCaR- or Catur-style) is the obvious learned competitor.
- Unequal tuning: baselines kept published settings while PageScore was tuned on validation streams
  (§7).
- Every number is divided by Clock's, yet the kernel's Clock is not textbook
  (kernel/vmpage.c:172-190): its index hand over a load-ordered list skips the page after each
  victim, since the victim is removed and the new page appended. Obs. 1 suggests it is close to LRU;
  describe it and check textbook Clock.

W3. The effect is modest, "matches ARC" is not shown, and the worst cases are on training workloads.
- At 5% memory the gain over Clock is not significant, but ARC's is (Table 8): ARC vs Clock 0.947
  [0.92, 0.98]; PageScore vs Clock 0.972 [0.90, 1.08]; PageScore vs ARC 1.027 [0.97, 1.13].
- "Matches" ARC and LHD rests on intervals that include 1 (vs ARC at 20%: [0.83, 1.00]); that is not
  equivalence. Test equivalence with a stated margin, or say "no significant difference".
- Writes at 10% (Table 11): PageScore writes 5% more pages than Clock, ARC and LHD about 12% fewer
  (1.05 vs 0.88/0.89; matmul 4.75×, SpMV 1.75×), because the learned weights evict written pages
  sooner (§3.3), against clean-first practice. On write-sensitive storage ARC wins.
- PageScore has 1.72× Clock's faults on matmul at 5% (Table 9) and 1.22× on graph at 20% (Table 10),
  both training workloads. RQ4's "fails only in a few specific cases, each with a clear cause"
  undersells a 72% regression.

W4. Platform and scale are far below the current bar.
- xv6 under QEMU: one core, one program at a time, 2–824 resident pages (at most 3.3 MB), no page
  cache, huge pages or multicore. Comparable work: cache_ext on Linux 6.6 with 1–10 GiB cgroups,
  xTier on Linux 6.11, LRB with 64 GB–1 TB caches.
- 5–20% of each program's pages is deep thrashing; nothing above 20% is tested.
- Many runs are short or degenerate, yet each workload weighs 1/14 of the headline: some app runs
  cause only 37–50 evictions; Twitter replays (cut to about 1,000 keys and 40,000 requests) cause
  859–6,189; sort runs with 2–24 resident pages, matmul with 2, 5 or 10. Appendix B: the simulator's
  start state changes one model's faults 14-fold on a stream.
- The Linux evidence is two Valgrind traces in the simulator, and PageScore loses to Clock on Redis
  (1.106, Table 25).
- "Inside a real kernel" (RQ2) overstates a teaching kernel under emulation; say "in the xv6
  kernel".

W5. Overhead and end-to-end benefit are modeled, not measured.
- Decision time is QEMU's instruction count times ns per instruction from a host user-space
  benchmark (tools/ml2/native_timing.c) that omits the page-table walk, accessed-bit clearing, TLB
  flush and locks, with data in a hot static array. 0.047 ns per instruction is about 4.8
  instructions per cycle at 4.4 GHz, a hot-cache figure; a real kernel's 32 random page records and
  page-table entries will miss in cache and TLB. The 1 ns "slow core" is assumed.
- No per-decision latency distribution in a real kernel and no runtime for any workload; Baleen
  (FAST'24) criticizes miss-ratio-only results.
- Only an SSD (63 µs per read) and a hard disk (8.9 ms). Many Linux systems swap to compressed RAM
  (zram/zswap, the default on Android, ChromeOS and Fedora) or far memory, at roughly 1–10 µs per
  fault. Table 16's break-even fault costs are 3–35 µs on most workloads for scoring every page, and
  roughly half for the 32-page sample at small sizes, so the pay-off claim probably fails there.
- Clearing accessed bits needs TLB shootdowns across cores; one emulated core hides this, and §7
  leaves the TLB flush out of the cost.
- PageScore adds 40 bytes per resident page and 12 per swap slot (§4); Linux's whole struct page is
  64 bytes. Show the mapping onto Linux state (MGLRU generation bits, workingset shadow entries) and
  the cost of fewer bits.

W6. No safeguard against a wrong model.
§3.3: "Nothing switches PageScore off while it runs"; the fallback fires only on invalid choices.
Accepted ML-for-OS papers all handle mispredictions (rate-based revert, shadow default policy, safe
action space, out-of-distribution detection), and there is no wrong-model or noise-injection test.
With 1.72× and 1.22× regressions and the Redis loss, reviewers will ask first. Guardrails (HotOS'25)
is in references.bib but uncited.

W7. Gaps in the ML methodology.
- Belady labels need full access traces; there is no story for labels or retraining in production
  (e.g., refaults as delayed labels), the crux of deployment.
- Distribution shift: training decisions come from LRU replay, but deployed, PageScore decides what
  stays. The DAgger evidence is one PageRank stream (Table 20); §5.3 gives "no consistent gain"
  without numbers.
- Missing training details: the ridge λ, how often decisions are recorded ("at some of the
  evictions"), the scaling σⱼ, how never-reused pages are labelled, and one list of the five
  features (idle, age, dirty, refaults, rdist).
- Selection risk: features come from a large search (300 linear models, Table 20); the sample size
  32 was first picked on test streams (§3.3); probation's sensitivity isn't shown in the kernel.
- "Time" is counted in evictions, so idle and age depend on the fault rate, hence on the policy and
  memory size.

W8. Some claims go beyond the evidence.
- The conclusion blames "what the kernel can see and how little time it can spend", not learning.
  But models given full-trace features reach only 0.847–0.856 of Clock's faults against Belady's
  0.56–0.59 (Table 23), and slower, larger models gain nothing (§5.3), so neither is shown to be the
  limit.
- "The kernel already sees enough": enough to tie an adapted ARC, not to approach optimal. Say
  which.
- §7 says cache_ext policies "use 10 to 20 pages per eviction", but the cache_ext paper's examples
  use 32 candidates (its LFU picks 32 of the first 512 folios), and the repo's only source is the
  handoff text. Verify or cut.
- The abstract's third difficulty, "every eviction changes later faults", is true of every cache.


MLSYS 2027 RISKS

- Scope: the 2027 topics name nothing on OS memory management, and LLM tools screen for scope. None
  of the 233 papers in the 2024–2026 proceedings is on OS paging; accepted ML-for-systems papers run
  at fleet scale (LAVA, Catur). Lead with the learning problem.
- Appendix: "reviewers are not required to read" it, yet arguments rest on Tables 8 (intervals), 11
  (writes), 13 (cost growth), 14–16 (break-even), 22 (splits) and 23 (information vs model size).
- AI policy: the disclosure is mandatory; MLSys adds one AI-generated review per paper and screens
  for fabricated references. Ours says the agent "ran and monitored the experiments, computed the
  statistics"; add how the authors checked them.
- Anonymization: the red repository and dataset placeholders need anonymous mirrors.


MISSING RELATED WORK

- Should cite: for sampling, LRB (credit sampling and label), LHD, Hyperbolic caching, Redis's
  approximated LRU, Psounis & Prabhakar; CAR and CLOCK-Pro as accessed-bit baselines; PageFlex
  (ATC'25) and Guardrails (HotOS'25), both in references.bib but uncited; Heimdall (EuroSys'25),
  FetchBPF (ATC'24), and Baleen (FAST'24) on end-to-end metrics.
- Context: MGLRU decision overhead and run-to-run variation (IISWC'24, ROSS'25); tiering and
  offloading (TPP, Memtis, Colloid, Nomad, TMO); other learned replacement (MUSTACHE, ML-CLOCK,
  Raven, Cold-RL); LLM-written heuristics (PolicySmith, Vulcan), since the 2027 topics name
  "LLM-based … system optimization".


MINOR ISSUES

- The title asks a question whose answer is "Partly".
- Say "geometric mean", and define a fault (swap faults plus zero-fill).
- Report stream lengths and evictions per workload; flag runs with 2 resident pages.
- The kernel form of LeCaR (Clock vs a frequency count) isn't really LeCaR; present it as a failed
  adaptation.
- Give the larger models' "50–400×" cost in microseconds.
- Move the per-stream simulator–kernel spread (up to 72%) into the main text; features were chosen
  in the simulator.


QUESTIONS FOR THE AUTHORS

1. How does PageScore compare with CAR, CLOCK-Pro and a sampled LHD, using the same accessed bits?
2. How much of the gain comes from sampling plus probation alone?
3. What does a decision cost in a real kernel, with cold caches and multicore TLB shootdowns?
4. How would labels be collected for a new workload without full traces?
5. What happens at 30–50% memory, and with swap to compressed RAM or far memory?
6. Does the 9% fault reduction show up in any workload's runtime?


WHAT WOULD MOST IMPROVE THE PAPER BEFORE 30 OCT

1. Wording and credit (hours): credit LRB and LHD; sharpen the difference from LearnedCache; replace
   "matches" with "no significant difference" or test equivalence; fix the conclusion, "real kernel"
   and RQ4; mention writes against ARC; fix the cache_ext figure; describe the Clock variant.
2. From existing data (GawwyG, hours): a per-stream best-of-classical oracle; equivalence bounds
   against ARC and LHD; break-even at 1–10 µs fault costs; the headline without the tiny runs.
3. New runs (GawwyG, 1–3 days): sampled LHD (64), CAR and CLOCK-Pro at 10% memory; the sampled
   max-idle-plus-probation baseline; textbook Clock and 30–50% memory in the simulator.
4. A safeguard and a wrong-model test (2–3 days).
5. Later: Linux (cache_ext batch scoring for file pages, or a hook in anonymous-page reclaim) with
   runtime results.
