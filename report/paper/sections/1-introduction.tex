% --- Move 2: how the OS does it today, and the measured failure --------------
Operating systems choose which page to evict with recency heuristics built on the
hardware accessed bit: \clock and its descendants~\citep{corbato1968paging,carr1981wsclock},
and in Linux the active/inactive lists and the multi-generational LRU~\citep{linux_mglru}.
These heuristics leave a large gap to the optimum. On B-tree, key-value and graph
workloads, \belady's offline policy~\citep{belady1966study} incurs 43--54\% fewer faults
than \clock, and \clock is within 2\% of exact LRU on five of the six training
workloads (\S\ref{sec:bg-headroom}). Stronger classical policies such as
ARC~\citep{megiddo2003arc}, LHD~\citep{beckmann2018lhd} and
S3-FIFO~\citep{yang2023s3fifo} were designed for caches that see every hit; in the form
a kernel can run, the best of them, ARC, incurs about 5\% fewer faults than \clock
(Table~\ref{tab:teaser}).

% --- Move 3: why prior learned approaches do not solve it --------------------
\plan{One or two sentences per family. Learned caching for CPU caches, CDNs and storage
(\citealp{jain2016hawkeye,shi2019glider,liu2020parrot,song2020lrb,vietri2018lecar,rodriguez2021cacheus})
observes every access, runs in floating point and is judged per request. A kernel sees
none of that: it sees faults and the accessed/dirty bits it samples. No archival MLSys
paper applies ML to kernel page replacement; the accepted ML-for-OS papers work above
the kernel (VM allocation, NUMA placement, storage tiering).}

% --- Move 4: the question and the insight ------------------------------------
This paper asks whether a learned page-replacement policy is \emph{implementable} at
operating-system level: with only the signals a kernel observes, without floating point,
on the eviction path, and without knowing which program it serves. Our answer is a
qualified yes: \textbf{the kernel already sees enough}. Accessed-bit history and the
refault bookkeeping kernels already keep carry the signal a learned policy needs; a
small linear score over them, computed in whole numbers, beats \clock inside the kernel
and is level with the strongest classical policy a kernel can run. Larger models do not
pay for their cost.

% --- Move 5: challenges -> techniques (the paper's spine; see paper-state.md) --
\plan{Five challenges, each measured, each mapped to one technique of
\S\ref{sec:design} and one ablation of \S\ref{sec:eval}:
(C1) the kernel never sees accesses $\rightarrow$ features from accessed/dirty-bit scans
and refault bookkeeping (\S\ref{sec:design-features});
(C2) no floating point and a per-eviction budget $\rightarrow$ a whole-number linear
score; heavier models also converted to whole numbers for comparison
(\S\ref{sec:design-int});
(C3) decisions compound, and naively sampled training data teaches the policy to evict
the page being streamed through $\rightarrow$ decision-point training with
recency-stratified recording, plus probation for new pages (\S\ref{sec:design-train});
(C4) a wrong model can be worse than \clock $\rightarrow$ validation-only selection, a
validated victim, and a fallback guard (simulator prototype; \S\ref{sec:design-wrong});
(C5) scoring every page is slow $\rightarrow$ scoring 32 sampled candidates
(\S\ref{sec:design-cost}).}

% --- Move 6: contributions ----------------------------------------------------
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A learned page-replacement policy that runs inside a kernel}: a whole-number
  linear score over kernel-visible signals, with per-process weights and sampled
  scoring, plus five heavier models (CNN, MLP, set-attention, boosted and ranking trees)
  in whole-number form (\S\ref{sec:design}, \S\ref{sec:impl}).
\item \textbf{A study of what a learned eviction policy needs.} Oracle inputs help
  richer models by 2--5\% on the workloads they were trained on and make them fail on
  real application traces; with kernel-visible inputs, more capacity does not pay
  (\S\ref{sec:bg}, \S\ref{sec:eval-info}, \S\ref{sec:eval-ablate}).
\item \textbf{An in-kernel evaluation against strong classical policies}: 14 workloads
  (eight never used in training), three memory sizes, 2,375 kernel runs and 95\%
  intervals, against \clock and kernel forms of ARC, LHD and S3-FIFO, with exact
  instruction counts for every policy's decision (\S\ref{sec:eval-kernel},
  \S\ref{sec:eval-cost}, \S\ref{sec:eval-robust}).
\item \textbf{A paging platform and dataset for xv6}: demand paging and swap, and
  complete read/write-marked reference strings checked against independent models of
  every program (\S\ref{sec:impl}, Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

  Kaggle runs are part of D2}
\end{itemize}

% --- Move 7: headline results with baseline named ----------------------------
At 10\% memory the linear score incurs 8.6\% fewer faults than \clock (95\% interval
2--15\%); at 5\% and 20\% it is better on average (2.1\% and 11.3\% fewer) but the
intervals include no change. Against ARC it moves from 3.5\% more faults at 5\% to
10.1\% fewer at 20\%, and no interval excludes a tie. It fails on specific workloads
(matrix multiply at 5\%, sparse matrix--vector product, graph with the global weights;
\S\ref{sec:eval-loss}). Choosing a victim costs 22,803 instructions among 68
candidates, 55$\times$ \clock and about twice ARC; sampling 32 candidates holds the cost
at about 11,700 instructions whatever the memory size. Assuming a fault cost we did not
measure, the per-workload linear score pays for itself on a hard disk and roughly breaks
even on fast flash (\S\ref{sec:eval-cost}).
